District Story · Winter Park

Winter Park

A park built for the winter, drawn on paper in 1881, and still legible in every street you walk today.

Chapter One · 1858 to 1887

A Town Drawn on Paper

Before Winter Park was a town, it was a shoreline. In 1858, David Mizell Jr. and his family bought eight acres between Lakes Virginia, Mizell and Berry, and the settlement that grew around their homestead took the plain name Lake View. In 1870 it renamed itself Osceola and opened a post office. Then, in 1880, the South Florida Railroad reached the lakes, and the land along the line caught fire with buyers.

The modern town starts with a February visit. Loring Chase first saw the area in February 1881 and went home to Massachusetts with a plan. With his boyhood friend Oliver Chapman, he bought 600 acres for $13,000, and by late summer the two had platted a town out of the pine flatwoods: a grid of streets, a 10 acre park set beside the railroad tracks, a lakeside site reserved for a grand resort hotel, and Hannibal Square, a Black residential district for the domestic servants and grove workers the resort economy would run on. The Winter Park History Museum describes their goal as a planned community of winter homes for wealthy Northerners, and calls the result the first planned community in Florida.

The name came out of an informal conversation. The city tells it directly: kicking names around, Chase and Chapman decided they wanted something about a park in winter. Winter Park is not a metaphor. It is a literal description of the product being sold, a park-like town for the winter season, and the founders said so themselves.

In October 1887, Winter Park incorporated as a town, and the deciding votes came from Hannibal Square: rallied by Gus Henderson, its residents voted to bring their neighborhood inside the town limits, and the History Museum records that their vote carried the election. The town became the City of Winter Park in 1925.

Then and Now

The 10 acre park drawn beside the tracks in 1881 is Central Park today, still holding the west side of Park Avenue under the oak canopy. The drawing was never revised. It just grew up around itself.

Chapter Two · The Resort Era

The Grand Hotel on the Lake

Every planned resort needs its palace, and Winter Park's rose on the shore of Lake Osceola. The Seminole Hotel opened on New Year's night 1886, and the city's history lists its wonders like a sales brochure that happens to be true: gas lights, steam heat, an orchestra, a formal dining room and long covered porches built for watching the lake. Northern guests arrived by rail, took the winter air, and wrote home about Florida as if they had found a loophole in the seasons.

The resort era peaked early and burned bright. The Seminole was the town's proof of concept, the machine that turned a plat on paper into a society season. Around it, winter residents built their houses along the lakes and named the new streets after the states they had left behind, a habit the street signs still confess today.

Then and Now

The Seminole's long porches are gone, but the lake it faced is still the town's front room. The Albin Polasek Museum keeps a sculptor's home, studio and sculpture gardens on Lake Osceola today, and the Scenic Boat Tour crosses the same water the hotel guests watched from the porch rail.

Chapter Three · 1885

A College Older Than the City

Winter Park was four years along as a plat and not yet a town when it landed a college. In late April 1885, the Florida Congregational Association chose Winter Park as the site of what would become Florida's first four year college. The school was organized in Sanford on April 28, 1885, and classes began in Winter Park that November, two years before the town around it incorporated.

It was named for Alonzo Rollins, a Chicago industrialist whose leadership gift of land and money came to $50,000, the fortune that turned a mission into a campus. Founded by New England Congregationalists to bring liberal arts education to the Florida frontier, the college was designated Florida's oldest college by a 2004 Florida House resolution. It earned the title honestly: it has simply been here the longest, predating the city charter, the freeze and the century.

Then and Now

The campus still sits where the founders put it, at the lake end of town, and the guide's Scenic Boat Tour still glides past it. A college planted in a four year old resort plat turned out to be the most permanent thing either founder built.

Chapter Four · 1932

Prayer and Drama, Side by Side

In 1926, Frances Knowles Warren and her sister Mabel Knowles Gage gave Rollins $100,000 in memory of their father, a founder of the college: $75,000 to build a chapel and $25,000 to maintain it. President Hamilton Holt hired Ralph Adams Cram, America's foremost church architect, designer of Princeton's chapel and of work at St. John the Divine. The final bill came to $250,000, over $4 million in today's money, spent in the depths of the Great Depression while the faculty took pay cuts to keep the college alive.

Holt had an argument to win. The students had just voted to end mandatory daily chapel, and he wanted religion back at the center of campus life. Then he read that modern drama was born from Easter Mass services, a child of the church, and the plan doubled: build the new theater beside the chapel, set a cloistered garden between them, link the two with loggias, and let chapel and theater stand in conversation. The Annie Russell Theatre was designed by Richard Kiehnel, and Cram, despite the different authorship, said the pair formed one composition.

Knowles Memorial Chapel, photographed for the Historic American Buildings Survey. Library of Congress, public domain.

Both buildings were dedicated on the same day, March 29, 1932. In 1936, Franklin D. Roosevelt received an honorary degree inside the chapel. The college historian calls the place, affectionately, a white elephant: a quarter of a million Depression dollars spent to win an argument with the student body. The building won. The argument did not. But the chapel became the picture of Rollins all the same, and it stands on the National Register of Historic Places.

Then and Now

Chapel and theater still face each other across their cloistered garden on the Rollins campus, prayer and drama side by side exactly as they were dedicated in 1932. The tower in the photograph is still the one that rises over the lake end of town.

Chapter Five · 1894 to 1904

The Freeze, and the Man Who Replanted the Town

The resort town ran on citrus money and mild weather, and in the winter of 1894 the weather collected its debt. Freezes in December 1894 and again in February 1895, back to back, destroyed the citrus crop and the town's finances with it. Groves died where they stood. The boom that had drawn a railroad, a college and a grand hotel stalled in the cold.

Recovery arrived in 1904 in the person of Charles Hosmer Morse, the Chicago industrialist behind the Fairbanks Morse Company. Morse settled in Winter Park, bought hundreds of acres, and set about endowing the town he had adopted, deeding land for Central Park and giving ground for a golf course and its clubs. Where Chase and Chapman had drawn the town, Morse landscaped it, and his name settled onto its main boulevard.

Then and Now

Morse's name rides the boulevard, and it is over the door of the Charles Hosmer Morse Museum of American Art on Park Avenue, where the guide sends you for the world's most comprehensive collection of Tiffany, chapel interior included. The park he deeded still anchors the avenue he helped save.

Chapter Six · The Working Water

Ditches That Became the Main Event

The prettiest ride in Winter Park started as industrial plumbing. In the late 19th century, canals were dug by hand between the lakes, Maitland, Osceola, Virginia, Mizell and Minnehaha, to float harvested logs down to the sawmills. The chain of lakes was a work yard before it was a postcard.

In 1938, the work yard changed jobs. The Scenic Boat Tour began carrying passengers through the same cuts, the Venetian Canal and the Fern Canal among them, and marketed Winter Park as the Venice of America. The pitch was shameless and the product was real: still water, the college gliding past, big houses on the shore, and canals narrow enough to brush the ferns.

Then and Now

The tour never stopped. It is in the guide today, still crossing the chain of lakes, still threading the logging ditches past Rollins College and the storied mansions of the shore. The prettiest boat ride in town is a sawmill shortcut that refused to retire.

The Index in the Street Signs

How the Streets Got Their Names

A planned town confesses its plan in its street signs. Winter Park's name the park it was sold on, the industrialist who rescued it, the states its winter people came from, and the men who laid out its roads.

  • Park Avenue: named for the park it borders. The 1881 plat set a 10 acre park beside the railroad tracks, and Central Park still sits on the avenue's west side.
  • Morse Boulevard: named for Charles Hosmer Morse, the Chicago industrialist behind the Fairbanks Morse Company, who made his home here in 1904, bought hundreds of acres and deeded Central Park to the town. His name is also on the Charles Hosmer Morse Museum of American Art.
  • New York Avenue: a state name. The History Museum records that winter residents named streets after the states they left.
  • Pennsylvania Avenue: a state name, from the same History Museum list of streets named for the home states of northern winter residents.
  • Minnesota Avenue: a state name, from the same list.
  • Georgia Avenue: a state name, from the same list.
  • Virginia Avenue: a state name, from the same list.
  • Fairbanks Avenue: named for Franklin Fairbanks, whose father and uncle founded the E. and T. Fairbanks scale company in 1824. Franklin became its president in 1888 and was a seasonal resident and property owner in Winter Park.
  • Comstock Avenue: for William C. Comstock, a Chicago grain merchant who in 1885 became a director of the Winter Park Land Company, in charge of laying out roads, railways and buildings, and a charter trustee of Rollins College.

Two famous names stay undocumented, and this page will not invent them. No source consulted says who named New England Avenue, likely as it sounds. Interlachen, often explained as between the lakes or as a nod to Interlaken in Switzerland, is repeated as a likelihood by real estate writing, not documented by a city or museum source. Knowles Avenue likely honors Francis Bangs Knowles, the Rollins benefactor behind Knowles Hall in 1886 and Knowles Memorial Chapel in 1932, but the street's own naming record was not found.

The Story, On Foot

Walk It Today

The whole story fits inside a slow afternoon. Start where the plat started, at Central Park, and walk Park Avenue the way the town was drawn: coffee on the avenue, a table at Prato, the Tiffany chapel interior waiting inside the Morse Museum, and Casa Feliz, the historic house museum, keeping the avenue's north end.

Then give the town back its water. The Polasek Museum's sculpture gardens sit on Lake Osceola, and the Scenic Boat Tour still leaves through the old logging canals, past the college that predates the city, across the lake the stereoscopic cameras photographed when the town was new. Nothing on the route is a reconstruction. It is the drawing, still inhabited.
